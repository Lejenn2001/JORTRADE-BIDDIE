Pressure-Testing the Dual Entry Concept
1. Pros and cons of showing Primary Entry + Secondary Entry
Pros:

Solves the problem at the right moment. The trader sees both paths before the move starts, not after. This is fundamentally different from labeling something "Momentum Confirmed" after the fact — by then, the opportunity has already passed. A trader looking at NBIS PUT in real time would see "Primary: $120 / Secondary: $123–124" and can make an informed decision about whether to wait for $120 or participate at $123.

Honest about how markets actually work. Not every trade retraces to the textbook entry. Experienced traders know this. Showing a participation zone acknowledges reality without pretending the system can predict perfect entries every time.

Preserves the precision entry as the recommended path. The primary entry stays prominent. The secondary zone is clearly labeled as a less ideal but valid alternative. The system isn't weakening its recommendation — it's giving the trader a choice.

Makes the signal more useful in fast-moving markets. If NBIS is dropping from $124 and a trader is watching in real time, they currently see "Entry: $120" and wait. Price hits $122, reverses, and they missed it. With a secondary zone at $123–124, they had a framework to participate.

Cons:

More information on an already dense card. The signal cards already show entry, target, stop, strike, expiry, conviction score, thesis, flow bias, and contract details. Adding a second entry zone makes the card busier, especially on mobile.

Potential for "paradox of choice." A beginner trader might freeze: "Do I wait for $120 or enter at $123? Which is the real entry?" The current system is simple: one entry, one answer. Two entries introduces decision-making that a beginner-friendly platform is trying to reduce.

The secondary zone needs its own stop/target math. If you enter at $123 instead of $120 on a PUT, your risk/reward ratio changes. The stop might be the same, but your entry is worse, so your potential loss is larger relative to your potential gain. The system would need to either recalculate risk/reward for the secondary entry or display a warning that risk is different. Otherwise, the displayed stop and target were designed for the $120 entry, not the $123 entry.

Harder to backtest and validate. You can objectively measure whether price hit $120 (yes/no). Measuring whether price "entered the $123–124 zone with sufficient momentum" requires defining what "sufficient" means — duration, volume, direction persistence. This is subjective in a way the current system isn't.

2. Does this solve the real problem better than Momentum Confirmed?
Yes, for the trader in real time. No, for system accuracy tracking.

Here's the distinction:

Momentum Confirmed (my prior recommendation)	Dual Entry (your concept)
When it helps	After the move happened	Before the move starts
Who it helps	The analyst reviewing performance	The trader making a live decision
What it solves	"The system missed it" perception	"I don't know if I should enter" problem
Accuracy impact	None — it's a separate reporting lane	Introduces a second entry path with different accuracy
Actionability	Zero — it's retrospective	High — it gives the trader a framework to act
Your instinct is right: the real problem isn't labeling after the fact — it's giving the trader a reason to act in the moment. A trader staring at NBIS dropping doesn't care that the system will later call it "Momentum Confirmed." They care about whether they should get in now.

Momentum Confirmed is a better analytics solution. Dual Entry is a better product solution.

However, they're not mutually exclusive. Dual Entry tells the trader what to do. Momentum Confirmed tracks what happened. The strongest version of the system would eventually have both.

3. Specific risks
User confusion:
The biggest risk. Right now, entry is one number. Users don't have to think. With two entries, the natural question is: "Which one should I use?" If the system doesn't clearly communicate the difference — and the trade-off of each — users will either:

Always use the secondary (easier to hit) and get worse results
Ignore the secondary and wonder why it's there
Feel overwhelmed by too many numbers
Mitigation: The secondary entry should be visually secondary — smaller text, muted color, positioned below the primary entry. Labeled something like "Momentum Zone" or "Participation Range" with a one-line explanation: "Less ideal entry — use if price is already moving."

Lower-quality entries:
By definition, the secondary entry is at a worse price. For the NBIS PUT example: entering at $123 instead of $120 means you're $3 closer to your stop and $3 further from your target. On a $5 wide trade, that's a massive difference in risk/reward. Some trades that would be 2:1 reward-to-risk from the primary entry become 1:1 or worse from the secondary entry.

Worse win rate on the secondary path:
I'd estimate the secondary path would have a 15–25% lower win rate than the primary path. Reason: you're entering with less confirmation. The primary entry exists because it represents a level where the thesis is confirmed (price broke below VWAP, price hit support, etc.). The secondary zone is "price is moving in the right direction but hasn't confirmed yet." Some of those moves will reverse — they were just normal intraday volatility, not the start of a real move.

Analytics complexity:
You'd need to track:

Primary entries and their outcomes (existing system)
Secondary entries and their outcomes (new)
Blended vs. separated win rates
Whether signals that triggered secondary entries also eventually triggered primary entries (how often does waiting for the better price actually work?)
This is manageable but requires UI for the user to understand what they're looking at. If you show one blended win rate, it drops. If you show two separate win rates, users need to understand why they're different.

Trust in the displayed entry:
Currently, when the system says "Entry: $120," users trust it as the system's best recommendation. Adding a secondary entry at $123 could subtly undermine that: "If $123 is also valid, why is $120 the recommended entry? Is the system not confident in $120?" The primary entry needs to remain clearly positioned as the better trade — the secondary is a compromise, not an equal alternative.

4. How should the secondary path be introduced?
Yes to all three of your proposals. Here's why each matters:

Display-only guidance at first:
The secondary zone should appear on the signal card as informational text only. The system does not track whether the trader entered at the secondary level. It doesn't change signal status. It's purely: "If you can't get $120, the $123–124 area is a reasonable participation zone." This lets you see how users respond without any system risk. Do traders find it useful? Do they understand it? Do they ignore it?

Tracked separately from the primary entry:
When you eventually start tracking it, it must be its own column/field — not overwriting the primary entry tracking. Something like secondary_entry_reached, secondary_entry_price, secondary_entry_at. This way, you can run both systems side by side and compare outcomes. "Of signals where both entries triggered, how did outcomes differ? Of signals where only the secondary triggered, what percentage still hit target?"

Excluded from headline win rate until proven:
Absolutely. The headline win rate should remain "precision entry win rate" — the number users have learned to trust. Secondary entry outcomes should appear in a separate section, maybe labeled "Momentum Entry Results" or "Participation Zone Accuracy." Only after you have enough data (50–100+ secondary entries with outcomes) should you consider whether to blend them, and even then, only if the secondary win rate is within an acceptable range (say, 65%+ for a system currently running ~91%).

5. Which approach is better if the goal is actionable signals, not just accurate reporting?
Dual Entry is the better product for a trader. Here's the honest comparison:

Momentum Confirmed says: "You were right, but you missed it." This is useful for confidence and system credibility, but it doesn't help the trader make money. By the time a signal is labeled Momentum Confirmed, the move is over.

Dual Entry says: "Here's where to get in ideally, and here's where to participate if the move starts without you." This helps the trader make a decision in real time. It turns a "watch and wait" situation into "here's your framework for action."

For a platform whose tagline is "Trade With Confidence," giving the trader a participation framework is more valuable than telling them after the fact that the system's thesis was correct.

However, the implementation risk of Dual Entry is higher. Momentum Confirmed is almost risk-free — it's just re-labeling an existing status. Dual Entry requires:

Defining how the secondary zone is calculated (VWAP to current price? Entry ± X%? Key level range?)
Designing the UI to show two entries without clutter
Eventually building separate tracking for secondary entries
Communicating clearly to users what the two entries mean
My recommendation for sequencing:

Phase 1 (lowest risk): Implement Momentum Confirmed — immediately improves how ran_without_entry signals are perceived. Display-only, analytics-only. Proves the concept that the system's directional calls are strong even when entry isn't hit.

Phase 2 (medium risk): Add the Secondary Entry / Participation Zone as display-only guidance on the signal card. No tracking, no analytics impact. Just information for the trader. Evaluate user response.

Phase 3 (after data): Start tracking secondary entries separately. Compare win rates. Decide whether to make the secondary path a first-class feature or keep it as guidance only.

This sequence lets you prove each layer before building the next, and you can stop at any phase if the data doesn't support continuing.
